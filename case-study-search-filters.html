Enhancing Search Filtering at Kroger

Two rounds of moderated research turned an overlooked filter panel into a visible, visual tool — and cut broad-query result sets by 80%.

Company Kroger (via Empathy.co)
Period 2020–2022
My role Product Designer — research, design, stakeholder comms
Platform Web & Mobile

Result

Filters went from overlooked to a primary way to navigate results.

Problem

Filters were buried, so customers gave up on them.

Kroger's filters sat in a sidebar panel, behaved inconsistently across web and mobile, and left out price and savings — core needs for grocery shoppers. Broad searches like "chicken" returned 1,000+ results, and customers retyped their query rather than filter.

My role

Research, design and alignment, end to end.

  • Ran two rounds of moderated research with the research team: a holistic search study and a competitive benchmark against Target and Walmart.
  • Turned findings into prioritised recommendations and presented them to the Product Manager and senior management.
  • Owned all design decisions, including directions I killed after testing.

Most participants would rephrase their search before touching a filter. The problem wasn't desire — it was discoverability.

What I designed

Three changes, each grounded in research.

  • Visual filters at the top of results. Image chips above the product grid. In testing, participants noticed and used them within seconds. Trade-off: a new component, and engineering work to surface category images dynamically.
  • Price, savings and dietary filters. Price and savings were the most-wanted filters, so I surfaced them in the visual strip rather than burying them in the panel.
  • Fewer, better results. With engineering, we tightened relevancy and capped broad queries, cutting 1,000+ results to ~160 and lifting add-to-cart conversion.

Outcome

Research gave us permission to be bold.

Features customers had ignored became features they used. The studies also showed filters were the highest-leverage area on the results page, which shaped 18 months of follow-up work.

Full research and prioritisation

Study 1 — Holistic search study

We expected to learn about queries and relevancy. Instead, most participants said they would refine their query before ever touching a filter — not by preference, but because the filters weren't visible or compelling enough to try. Once they did engage with filters or related tags, they used them repeatedly.

The study also surfaced the two filters customers wanted most but didn't have: price range (the #2 priority overall) and savings/promotions, essential for promotion-aware shoppers.

Study 2 — Refinement benchmark

A focused study of 10–15 Kroger customers comparing Kroger's refinement experience with Target and Walmart, on desktop and mobile. Three clear findings:

  • Visual filters were noticed immediately. Tags with images were described as highly relevant. Kroger's related tags had the right idea but weren't visually distinct enough.
  • Inconsistent behaviour caused confusion. Every participant was confused about why some filters allowed multi-select and others didn't.
  • When filters failed, customers went back to the search box. Participants used two words at most before trying to refine, so oversized result sets were the default.

Prioritising the work

I grouped recommendations into three tiers. The high-impact items became the core of the design brief:

  • High: combine visual navigation and tags into one prominent filter component at the top of results
  • High: make all filters behave consistently (multi-select, view selected, remove selected)
  • High: add price and savings as first-class filters
  • Medium: add dietary filters (organic, gluten-free, vegan)
  • Medium: reduce result sets for broad queries
